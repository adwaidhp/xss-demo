On Slowness

There is a kind of thinking that only happens when you are not trying to think. It arrives sideways, in the shower or on a long walk, unbidden and often inconvenient. We have built an entire civilization devoted to preventing it.

Speed is sold as freedom. You can have anything in two days, any answer in two seconds, any distraction in two taps. But speed has a cost that doesn't appear on the receipt: the slow processes — digestion, maturation, grief, understanding — do not compress. You cannot fast-forward fermentation.

Attention is finite and directional. Whatever you point it at grows; whatever you neglect atrophies. The modern economy has learned to point your attention at things that benefit it, not you. The antidote is not a detox or a retreat. It is a practice, daily and unglamorous, of choosing what to look at.

Slowness is not inefficiency. It is the condition under which certain things become possible. A conversation that goes nowhere useful. A book finished without urgency. An afternoon with no purpose. These are not failures of productivity. They are the substrate on which a life is grown.

None of this is new. Every generation rediscovers it, usually after burning out. The discovery doesn't feel like wisdom when you're inside it. It feels like stopping. It feels like loss. And then, slowly, it starts to feel like something else.
